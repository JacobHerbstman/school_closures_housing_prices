Permanent Public School Closures, Housing
Markets, and Neighborhood Change: A Source-
Verified Literature Review
Executive summary
The empirical literature does not support a single, universal sign for the effect of permanent public-school
closure on nearby housing demand. Instead, the best available evidence is consistent with a conditional
capitalization model:


       Closing a school tends to reduce nearby housing values when households lose a valued
       assignment right, convenient school access, or a neighborhood public good; closing can
       increase values when the school itself is perceived as weak, stigmatized, undesirable, or
       otherwise a local disamenity.


The strongest direct evidence for the first mechanism comes from the sudden 2012 closure of a well-
performing public laboratory school in Cedar Falls, Iowa. Rosburg, Isakson, Ecker, and Strauss estimate that
houses carrying the option to attend the school lost about 6.8%–7.2% of value, roughly $9,000 at the
sample mean, after an externally budget-driven closure. Because the school performed about as well as
nearby alternatives, their interpretation is that the capitalization reflected the option value of access plus
non-test-score school and neighborhood amenities, not simply measured academic quality. 1


The most direct evidence for the opposite mechanism is Schwartz and Voicu's New York City analysis. They
distinguish zoned from choice schools and find that a zoned-school closing was associated with an
immediate 2.6% decline in nearby prices, whereas a choice-school closing was associated with an
approximately 3.6% increase. The closing schools were, on average, lower-performing than schools that
remained open, and the positive effect of closing a choice school dissipated over time. Their interpretation
explicitly allows a weak school to constitute a local disamenity or negative signal. The design is unusually
informative conceptually but is less compelling causally than a sudden-closure quasi-experiment because
school openings and closings were endogenous urban events rather than randomly assigned shocks. 2


That contrast should not be read as establishing that closing low-performing urban neighborhood schools
generally raises prices. The urban evidence base is too thin for that conclusion. In particular, I did not locate
a modern transaction-level causal study that cleanly estimates the effect of a mass closure of low-
enrollment, low-performing zoned schools in weak-market, predominantly Black urban
neighborhoods while simultaneously examining both prices and housing-market quantities. National
Census-based studies instead suggest that closure is associated with lower neighborhood housing values
and, in highly segregated Black neighborhoods, with a greater probability and intensity of gentrification.
Pearman and Greene's national analysis reports an approximately 8-percentage-point increase in
gentrification probability in the most segregated Black neighborhoods exposed to closure; their
manuscript also finds a substantial relative loss of Black residents. These results do not prove that closure

The literature is also strongly price-centric. I located comparatively little credible closure-specific evidence
on the market's quantity margin. There is no comparably developed U.S. urban literature estimating effects
on listings, time on market, arm's-length sales volume, sale hazards, turnover, vacancy, tax
delinquency, foreclosure, rents, permitting, or redevelopment in a unified causal framework. This is an
important omission because a hedonic price regression conditions on a property actually being sold. If
closure causes families with children to stop listing, induces distressed owners to sell, changes the
composition of properties transacting, or freezes a weak market, the price effect among observed sales can
be quite different from the total housing-market effect.


The neighborhood-demographic evidence is more consequential than the number of studies might
suggest. Pearman and Greene find racialized gentrification effects concentrated in highly segregated Black
neighborhoods, while Arkansas rural-consolidation research reports population losses and lower assessed
property values after consolidation. In rural New York, other consolidation studies find heterogeneous and
even eventually positive capitalization, particularly for small or initially lower-income districts. Those rural
effects likely bundle school accessibility with tax savings, scale economies, district identity, travel
distance, and fiscal reorganization, and should not be transported mechanically to Chicago, Detroit,
Philadelphia, or other large-city closure contexts. 4


Building disposition deserves separate treatment. A closed school can become a vacant and stigmatized
structure, be demolished, reopen for community uses, host another school, or become market-rate
housing. Those pathways can radically alter subsequent capitalization. Yet reuse is itself selected:
developers acquire and convert buildings where market fundamentals, political support, financing, zoning,
and expected rents are favorable. A naïve comparison of reused versus vacant schools therefore confuses
the causal effect of reuse with the characteristics that made reuse feasible. Chicago research documents
both residential conversion of former school buildings and very uneven reuse trajectories; research on
Chicago crime also finds materially different post-closure patterns depending on whether a building
remained vacant, was repurposed, or merged with another school. 5


The Chicago finding is therefore especially striking. After targeted searches of combinations of “Chicago
2013 school closures” with housing prices, property values, transactions, real estate, Cook County, assessor,
recorder, Zillow, CoreLogic, repeat sales, vacancy, foreclosure, tax delinquency, gentrification, population, and
redevelopment, I located studies of student outcomes, crime, demographic change, vacant buildings, and
adaptive reuse, as well as Chicago research showing that school information can capitalize into housing
transactions. I did not locate, through August 27, 2026, a published paper, working paper, or
dissertation that directly estimates the causal effect of the 2013 CPS mass closures on parcel-level
housing transaction prices or sales activity. This is a documented search conclusion, not a claim that
such a study cannot exist in an unindexed archive. 6

able to inspect the original paper; original publisher record verified, where the publisher's original record
and abstract were available but the complete manuscript was not; and repository-abstract only, which I
list for completeness but do not use as core causal evidence. In particular, I do not manufacture coefficients
or methodological details that were absent from the accessible original materials.

Three features of this table are central.


First, the actual transaction-price closure literature is remarkably small. Rosburg et al., Schwartz and
Voicu, Bogart and Cromwell, and—if rural consolidation is included—Duncombe et al. are the clearest
studies directly connecting an actual closure/reorganization to observed sales. Merrall et al. use
transactions but simulate closure from a cross-sectional accessibility gradient rather than observing the
causal market response. 20


Second, the sign appears to depend on what the property owner is losing. In Rosburg, homeowners lose
an unusually attractive house-tied schooling option; in NYC, losing a zoned school is negative but the
closing of a weak choice school is positive. Rural consolidation adds fiscal economies and can therefore turn
positive after an initially negative adjustment. 21


Third, the national studies showing racialized neighborhood change use tract/neighborhood averages
rather than sales. They are indispensable for studying population and sorting, but they cannot tell us
whether an unchanged physical house would sell for a different price after closure in the same way a
repeat-sales design can. 9


What the closure literature says about mechanisms and outcomes
A useful model is to treat a school closing as a vector of shocks rather than a single treatment. At least nine
channels can operate at once, and different studies identify different combinations.




                                                       13
Loss of assigned-school access. This is the clearest reason to expect a negative capitalization effect. A
home can confer a legally or administratively valuable schooling option even when the family does not
currently use that school. The option has resale value because future buyers may have children, and it
provides insurance against other schooling alternatives. Rosburg et al. are unusually informative because
households in the relevant zone held an option to use MPLS or their ordinary Cedar Falls school. The
approximately 7% loss after MPLS disappeared therefore has a natural interpretation as lost school-option
and amenity value. Black's attendance-boundary research, discussed below, independently establishes that
assignment to a more desirable school can capitalize into otherwise similar houses. 22


Loss of a community anchor. School grounds can supply playgrounds, meeting space, recreation,
pedestrian activity, informal social networks and neighborhood identity even to households that do not
enroll a child. That makes physical proximity different from assignment rights. Merrall, Higgins and Páez
emphasize precisely this point: observed price-distance gradients may capture a bundle of school
accessibility, open space, neighborhood activity and related amenities rather than education alone. The
Rosburg finding also survives adjustment for measured school performance, which is consistent with—
although does not separately identify—these extra-educational benefits. 23


Removal of a weak, unpopular or stigmatized school. This mechanism flips the expected sign. A school
can convey information about neighborhood disadvantage, public disorder, peer composition or municipal
neglect. It can also generate traffic and noise without delivering an educational option buyers value.
Schwartz and Voicu provide the most direct empirical support for this possibility: NYC choice-school closings
were followed by higher nearby prices, while zoned-school closings were followed by lower prices; the
schools that closed were comparatively weak on measured academic indicators. Their results are consistent
with removal of a disamenity, although endogenous school closure prevents one from treating the +3.6%
coefficient as a clean universal causal effect of eliminating a “bad school.” 8


This distinction also explains why “low performing” and “low demand” are not interchangeable. Low
standardized test performance may make a school undesirable to affluent prospective homeowners, while
current families may nevertheless value its accessibility, trusted teachers, special programs, cultural
importance, after-school functions or stability. Conversely, a school can have respectable measured
achievement yet be inconvenient or unpopular. A strong research design needs multiple baseline
dimensions—test performance, enrollment demand, utilization, transfer applications, choice enrollment,
parent survey measures, school climate and receiving-school quality—rather than a single performance
label.


Replacement-school quality and accessibility can dominate the direct effect of closure. A family that loses
a school but is reassigned to a stronger school five blocks farther away experiences a different treatment
from a family reassigned two miles away to a similarly low-performing institution. Rural consolidation
makes this particularly obvious because larger travel distances arrive simultaneously with potential fiscal or
curricular benefits. The heterogeneous New York consolidation results—short-run losses followed by
recovery in many cases—are consistent with households gradually repricing that whole package rather than
simply valuing an empty building. 24


Closure as a signal of public disinvestment is especially difficult to separate from closure as a cause of
decline. Districts usually do not choose closings at random. Enrollment contraction, vacant housing,
foreclosures, population loss, falling birth cohorts and fiscal stress may precede the closure by years.
Buyers may interpret a closure announcement as new information that the public sector expects continued




                                                     14
decline. In that case, a negative event-study coefficient can partly be an informational response to future
neighborhood conditions rather than the mechanical value of the school itself. This is one reason national
tract-level associations require caution: Brazil's housing-value analysis and Pearman and Greene's
gentrification analysis deliberately control for observed neighborhood conditions, but unobserved
trajectories can remain. 25


The signaling mechanism also creates anticipation. The economically meaningful event may be the
publication of a utilization report, placement on a closure-candidate list, the board recommendation, final
board vote, last day of classes, reassignment of attendance boundaries, demolition, property sale, issuance
of a redevelopment permit, or completion of reuse. Treating “2013” as a single post indicator can average
over several distinct market-information events.


Vacancy, blight, crime and informal guardianship are plausible but not mechanically negative. A
shuttered school could reduce “eyes on the street,” leave a large building unmaintained and signal
abandonment. But closure can also eliminate student fights, traffic, noise, trespass associated with an
active campus, or crime concentrated around arrival and dismissal. Brazil's Chicago study is therefore
important: its crime findings do not fit a simple assumption that vacant closed-school buildings necessarily
raise nearby crime. 19


Redevelopment and reuse can produce an entirely new capitalization regime. Former schools can become
housing, offices, community centers or other schools. Chicago is a salient case: qualitative research has
documented conversions of former educational facilities into residential real estate and the tensions
between redevelopment, affordability, historic preservation and community goals. These examples
demonstrate a credible positive land-value mechanism while simultaneously illustrating why market
appreciation may benefit developers and incoming owners rather than families harmed by school
withdrawal. The causal problem is that high-value sites are precisely the sites most likely to attract
redevelopment.


Family out-migration and the loss of households with children can affect both housing demand and
neighborhood composition. Arkansas consolidation evidence finds local population reductions in treated
communities along with lower assessed values. The Chicago descriptive evidence is also suggestive: one
retrospective analysis using ACS data reported substantially larger population losses in majority-Black tracts
containing 2013 closed schools than in otherwise majority-Black tracts without closures, while explicitly
noting that this comparison cannot establish that closure caused the difference. 26


This channel deserves much more attention in cities because household type should respond more strongly
than total population. A closure may have little effect on young professionals, seniors or childless
households while substantially reducing the neighborhood's attractiveness to families with elementary-age
children. That could produce stable or even rising prices alongside a falling child population.


Gentrification and racial sorting provide the clearest reason that capitalization and welfare must be kept
separate. Pearman and Greene find the closure–gentrification relationship concentrated in the most
segregated Black neighborhoods and show that the substantive change is particularly connected to White
college-educated entry and Black population loss. Thus, a market response can plausibly look “positive” in a
hedonic regression while representing dispossession from the perspective of existing Black residents. 10

Taken together, the benchmark literature says more than “good schools raise home prices.” It says at least
four different objects are capitalized: an address-specific assignment right; information about school
quality or reputation; the availability of choice; and school facilities/accessibility. Those distinctions
matter because a closure changes each by a different amount.


For transaction-data research specifically, the dependent variable should ordinarily be the log of a carefully
screened arm's-length sale price, not an assessor value or Zillow-style estimated value. Local deed records
should exclude family transfers, nominal-price transactions, quitclaims, sheriff's deeds, bulk portfolio
transfers and other non-market conveyances. Where possible, the analyst should observe property




                                                      19
characteristics as of the transaction date rather than using today's assessor record for a sale a decade
earlier.


A basic hedonic model can be written conceptually as:


                     ln Pit = α + β(Closure exposurei × Postt ) + Xit γ + μg + τt + ϵit ,

where Pit is the sale price of property i, X contains property characteristics, μg is a fine spatial fixed effect
and τt is time. The problem is that this estimates the effect conditional on sale. Closure may itself affect
which houses sell.


A stronger transaction analysis therefore needs a second market-quantity equation. Every parcel, not just
sold parcels, can enter a monthly or quarterly panel with outcomes such as:


           Saleit ∈ {0, 1},       Listingsit ,       DOMit ,        F oreclosureit ,       Vacancyit .

The price and quantity results should be interpreted jointly. A 3% increase in the price of the small subset of
homes that continue to transact is not evidence of stronger market demand if sales volume simultaneously
collapses.


Repeat-sales models compare the same property across transactions, thereby absorbing all time-invariant
parcel and micro-location attributes. Duncombe, Yinger and Zhang's double-sales approach illustrates the
attraction of this method in school reorganization research. 17 Yet repeat-sales samples create their own
selection: houses that sell twice during the window may be systematically more liquid, more investor-owned
or more likely to be renovated. Renovations occurring between sales also violate the assumption that the
property is unchanged.


Parcel fixed-effects models can be even more useful when repeated assessor outcomes are available
because every parcel may be observed annually. Smith and Zimmer exploit this type of parcel panel for
assessments in Arkansas. 35 The disadvantage is that assessed value is administratively constructed and
can lag market conditions, be smoothed, or reflect assessment policy. In Cook County, this distinction is
especially important because assessment cycles and appeals can create changes unrelated to market
transactions.


Transaction event studies are preferable to a single pre/post coefficient when anticipation is possible. For
school closure, event time should be indexed separately to at least the credible announcement, final
closure decision and physical closure. A well-designed study can then ask whether prices begin moving
before students leave. A pre-announcement decline is evidence against interpreting a simple post-closure
drop as causal.


Boundary-based transaction designs are particularly promising because they can distinguish assignment
from physical proximity. Suppose two houses are 400 meters from a closed school but only one was inside
its attendance boundary. The first loses both assignment and a nearby institution; the second loses only the
institution. A before/after comparison of the discontinuity can therefore identify much more about
mechanism than a simple radial buffer.




                                                       20
Finally, buyer characteristics matter. Linking sales to HMDA mortgages, owner-occupancy exemptions or
other legally accessible data can reveal whether the same price effect reflects changed willingness to pay
among affluent borrowers, investor purchases, racial sorting or family demand. Crespin's Chicago work
demonstrates the value of treating housing capitalization and household sorting as joint outcomes. 33


Chicago mass-closure audit
Chicago Public Schools' 2013 episode is almost tailor-made for this research question because the district
simultaneously closed a large number of schools, overwhelmingly in Black and lower-income areas, while
publishing extensive information about enrollment, utilization, performance, proposed closures and
receiving schools. Yet the housing-market consequences remain much less studied than the student
consequences. The Consortium on Chicago School Research, for example, has evaluated where roughly
11,000 affected students subsequently enrolled and what happened academically, but those studies do not
constitute a housing-market analysis. 36

The result of that audit is best stated narrowly: I did not locate a transaction-level causal study that
estimates the effect of the 2013 CPS closures on nearby residential sale prices, sales probabilities,
sales volume, repeat sales, listings or time on market. I also did not locate a closure-specific Chicago
econometric paper centered on residential rents, tax delinquency or foreclosure. This is not proof of
absence; it is the result of the literature and repository search conducted through August 27, 2026.


What does exist is informative in pieces.


Crime and building disposition. Noli Brazil's Sociological Science article explicitly studies crime after the
2013 closures and differentiates vacant buildings, nonschool reuse and merger with an existing school. Its
results reject a simple assumption that school vacancy must mechanically increase nearby crime: reported



                                                      21
crime moved differently across disposition categories, including decreases around vacant and nonschool-
repurposed sites in the study's estimates. 19 This is an important mechanism study but not a property-
market estimate.


Vacant sites and uneven neighborhood distress. Investigative reporting several years after closure
documented that many remaining vacant school properties were concentrated in Black communities and
situated in already distressed neighborhood contexts. That evidence is useful for establishing disposition
and market context but does not supply a counterfactual for what those neighborhoods would have
experienced absent closure. 37


Population. A later Chicago Sun-Times analysis using ACS data reported that majority-Black census tracts
containing a closed school lost about 9.2% of residents between 2013 and 2018, compared with about
3.2% in majority-Black tracts without a 2013 closing. The analysis itself notes the crucial causal caveat: the
closed-school neighborhoods were systematically different and were often already losing population. That
result is therefore descriptive evidence worth explaining, not a closure treatment effect. 38


Redevelopment and residential reuse. Qualitative urban-geography and planning research has examined
former Chicago school sites converted or proposed for conversion to housing, including conflicts between
market-rate development, affordability, preservation and community goals. Keavy McFadden's work on
“school-hosted urban development” conceptualizes school-property conversion as part of real-estate-led
restructuring, while Ivis García examines a proposed teacher-housing/community-oriented reuse in a
gentrifying Chicago neighborhood. These studies are highly relevant to mechanism and political economy
but are not hedonic or DiD estimates of neighborhood prices.


Housing data exist. The Institute for Housing Studies at DePaul has constructed transaction-based Cook
County house-price measures and has separately analyzed Chicago's 2–4-unit stock using property,
foreclosure and vacant-land information. Those resources demonstrate that the lack of a located closure
study does not reflect an intrinsic absence of housing-market data. They also underscore a serious
identification problem: 2013 occurred during a highly uneven recovery from the foreclosure crisis, so
closure neighborhoods must not simply be compared with Chicago as a whole. 39


Chicago schools demonstrably affect transaction markets through information. Crespin's recent study
of school social-climate information uses Chicago housing transactions and finds capitalization and sorting
responses to school information. This strengthens the prior that a major school-access shock such as
closure could affect transactions while showing that a contemporary school–housing linkage is empirically
measurable. It still does not supply the missing closure estimate. 33

That gap matters precisely because Chicago is a difficult case for naïve methods. Closed schools were not
randomly selected. Enrollment decline and housing-market contraction are themselves related. Many
schools were geographically clustered, so nearby “control” neighborhoods can be exposed to another
closing. Receiving schools changed simultaneously. Some school buildings remained vacant and others
were reused. The broader South and West Side housing market was recovering from an extraordinary
foreclosure shock. And the district's policy was racialized geographically enough that citywide demographic
trends cannot substitute for a credible counterfactual. 40


Methodological critique
Hedonic transaction-price regressions are the natural starting point because they measure what buyers
actually paid for a particular house. They can flexibly control for square footage, property type, age, lot size,
condition, bedrooms, bathrooms and extremely fine spatial and temporal effects. Rosburg and Schwartz–
Voicu illustrate how informative such models can be when combined with policy timing. 41 Their central
weakness is twofold. First, the model is only as good as its closure counterfactual: a rich hedonic does not
make a politically selected closure exogenous. Second, it conditions on transactions, creating selective-sale
composition.


Selective sales are not a minor nuisance. Suppose families with children who would have sold decide not to
list after a closure, while investor-owned distressed properties continue to trade. Average observed price
can fall because the transaction mix changed even with no change in the latent price of a fixed house.
Conversely, rapid investor acquisition of cheap properties followed by renovation can produce apparent
appreciation unrelated to school demand. A serious closure study should therefore report sale counts and
sale probabilities alongside prices, estimate repeat-sale/parcel-fixed-effect specifications, and examine
observable characteristics of transacting properties over event time.


Repeat-sales and parcel fixed effects substantially improve control for fixed housing characteristics. They
are especially attractive in heterogeneous older housing stocks such as Chicago's. Duncombe, Yinger and
Zhang's use of repeated sales in New York consolidation research points in this direction. 17 The price of
the improvement is external validity: repeat sellers are not random. Renovation, conversion from two-flat to
single-family use, demolition/rebuild, foreclosure and investor flipping can all alter the supposedly fixed
asset between sales.


Difference-in-differences is attractive only if untreated properties provide a credible trajectory for what
would have happened to treated properties. The central threat in school-closure research is almost tailor-
made to violate that condition: districts tend to close schools in neighborhoods where children and
population are already declining. A single “closed versus open” DiD will therefore tend to attribute pre-
existing relative decline to closure. Event-study plots must show several pre-treatment years for both price
and market liquidity, not simply a statistically insignificant pre-period coefficient in one outcome.


The correct treatment date is also ambiguous. In mass closures the market can anticipate the event months
or years before the building actually closes. Analysts should distinguish exploratory closure reports,
candidate announcements, formal recommendations, board votes, final-day closure, boundary changes
and site redevelopment. A finding that prices fall only on the candidate announcement and not again when
the building closes would support an information/capitalization interpretation. A decline beginning after
the physical vacancy would point more toward community-anchor, blight or activity mechanisms.




                                                       23
Distance rings and spatial buffers are intuitive but surprisingly fragile. A “within one-half mile” treatment
compares properties just inside a researcher-chosen cutoff with properties just outside, even though both
may use the same school and perceive the same closure. Larger buffers worsen contamination when urban
schools are close together. In Chicago, a nominal control property may lie outside one closed school's 800-
meter radius but inside another's catchment or receive students displaced by closure. The treatment should
therefore be represented through several spatial dimensions simultaneously: old attendance assignment,
walking-network distance to the closed site, distance to alternative schools and exposure to other closed
sites.


Boundary discontinuity designs offer a stronger way to separate school assignment from neighborhood
location. Black's school-quality study demonstrates why: houses immediately across an attendance
boundary can share very similar local amenities but carry different school rights. 42 For closure, the most
useful design is not merely cross-sectional. It is a boundary-discontinuity difference-in-differences:
compare the price discontinuity around the former catchment boundary before and after closure. Then add
physical distance to the closed building. This can distinguish the lost assignment right from the loss of the
physical school/community asset.


Candidate-but-not-selected schools are probably the most valuable Chicago comparison group. In 2013
CPS evaluated a much larger universe of schools before settling on the final closing set. A control school
that was itself a credible closure candidate is more likely than an arbitrary open school to share low
utilization, enrollment decline, low performance, neighborhood disadvantage and public expectations. The
design is still not automatically causal: political mobilization, building condition, special programs, safety,
receiving-school availability and aldermanic or district priorities can determine which candidate is ultimately
selected. The analyst needs to reconstruct the selection process and demonstrate balance and pre-trends.


Propensity-score weighting or matched DiD can improve this candidate design by enforcing overlap in
baseline enrollment, utilization, racial composition, poverty, school performance, neighborhood
foreclosure, pre-closure price appreciation and sales volume. Smith–Zimmer and Duncombe–Yinger–Zhang
show how matching has been combined with longitudinal designs in consolidation research. 43 Matching,
however, only addresses observed differences. It should be viewed as a preprocessing device, not a
substitute for identification.


Synthetic control or synthetic DiD is most useful for a smaller number of prominent individual sites,
particularly unusual reuse events. For example, one could construct a synthetic neighborhood for a closed
school subsequently converted to apartments using weighted combinations of similar school
neighborhoods that track pre-closure prices, sales, vacancies and demographics. This is less attractive for
the entire 2013 Chicago treatment because dozens of closures make donor contamination pervasive. It is
also vulnerable when housing outcomes are noisy at very small geographies.


Most importantly, reuse is post-treatment and endogenous. A study should not estimate


                                  P rice = β1 Closed + β2 Reused + ⋯

and interpret β2 as “the causal effect of redevelopment.” Sites that attract reuse may have stronger
underlying markets, better architecture, transit access, zoning flexibility, more political support or lower
remediation costs. The better strategy is to model separate events—closure, reuse announcement, property
transfer, permit issuance, construction start, opening—and use credible matched or synthetic




                                                      24
counterfactuals for the reuse stage. Reuse should also be classified substantively: another school,
community use, affordable housing, market-rate housing, mixed use, demolition/green space and
continued vacancy are different treatments.


A further concern is simultaneous policy change. School choice may expand at the same time as
neighborhood schools close; receiving schools may obtain extra resources; transportation routes may
change; policing may be redeployed; housing subsidies, foreclosure interventions or tax-increment
financing may overlap; and a new development may be negotiated as part of site disposition. The treatment
is therefore often a policy package. Mechanism variables should not simply be added as ordinary regression
controls when they are themselves caused by closure, because that can introduce post-treatment bias.

estimate at least four classes of outcomes together:

The first design is the highest priority because it resolves both a substantive and methodological gap. A
Chicago price-only study would already add value, but a price-plus-sale-hazard design is substantially
better. The denominator for the sale outcome can be the full parcel universe, making it possible to detect
market thinning that a transaction-only regression cannot observe.


The second design is potentially the highest scientific payoff because it directly attacks mechanism. A
closure inside a former boundary changes assignment rights; a closure at equal physical distance on the
other side does not. Adding receiving-school quality then makes it possible to distinguish “I lost my local
school” from “my new school is worse/farther away.”


The third should follow the closure analysis, rather than being folded into it. Chicago's vacant and
redeveloped schools are not separate randomly assigned treatments. First establish the closure effect; then
treat the site's post-closure trajectory as a second event requiring its own counterfactual.


The fourth is indispensable for welfare interpretation. Pearman and Greene's national evidence makes it
entirely plausible that a closing can be followed by stronger housing demand while existing Black residents




                                                     31
decline. 10 A Chicago study that reports only a positive price coefficient without showing who remains and
who buys would therefore leave the most important policy question unanswered.

The resulting evidence hierarchy is therefore relatively clear. The most credible direct evidence says that
losing a valued, address-linked neighborhood-school option can materially depress nearby house prices.
  49 The best evidence for the opposite sign says that closure of a comparatively weak non-zoned/choice

school can coincide with higher prices, but that result is more exposed to closure-selection bias. 50
National neighborhood research warns that closures can participate in racialized gentrification and Black
population loss even when neighborhood demand subsequently strengthens. 10 Rural consolidation
research demonstrates that fiscal economies and longer-run re-equilibration can reverse short-run losses,
but it is a different treatment from the withdrawal of a single school in a large urban choice system. 24


For the substantive question—does closing a low-demand or low-performing neighborhood school
remove a disamenity, withdraw a public good, signal disinvestment, or create a redevelopment
opportunity?—the defensible conclusion as of August 2026 is that all four mechanisms are empirically
plausible, their relative strength depends on institutional context, and the existing U.S. urban
literature has not yet cleanly separated them. The most consequential unresolved case is precisely the
one represented by Chicago: low-demand schools, many of them weak on conventional metrics, embedded
in historically disinvested Black neighborhoods where school access, municipal withdrawal, foreclosure
recovery, population loss and redevelopment were changing together. A transaction-level Chicago study
that jointly estimates prices, liquidity and household sorting—and decomposes assignment loss from site
effects—would therefore make a substantial contribution rather than merely replicate an already-settled
literature.
