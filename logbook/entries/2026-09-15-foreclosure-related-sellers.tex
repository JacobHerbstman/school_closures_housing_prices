\section*{September 15, 2026: Foreclosure-related sellers and the 2010 price dip}
\textit{Drafted by Codex.}

The half-mile single-family sample does not show a higher share of
foreclosure-related sellers near treated schools in 2010. Among 116 treated
sales, 5 (4.3\%) name judicial-sale intermediaries, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac or
HUD as sellers, compared with 106 of 749 control sales (14.2\%). Adding bank
and mortgage-company names gives 31.0\% versus 40.2\%. All 2010 seller names
are reported. Before price and county-quality screens, the same geographic
and single-family comparison gives 4.8\% versus 14.4\% under the narrow
rule and 35.4\% versus 41.8\% under the broader rule.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../input/distress_seller_proxies.png}

These are seller-name proxies, not verified distress classifications.
The broader rule can include banks acting as land trustees, while either
rule can miss short sales and subsequent private resales. The treated broad
share is higher than control in 2011; crisis exposure is not uniformly
balanced. The 2010 comparison does not support the specific explanation
that a larger treated distressed-sale share generated that year's price
dip, but cannot rule out uneven foreclosure recovery or effects on unsold
properties. No price sample or regression has changed.

The public county export has no separate REO or short-sale flag. Detailed
MyDec deeds are reported for zero treated sales and only one control sale
in 2010. They therefore cannot supply the missing crisis classification.
Seller names are missing or UNKNOWN for 37.0\% of treated and 46.6\% of
control sales in 2015; the plotted name proxies omit that year. Other plotted
shares divide matches by sales with usable seller names. The accompanying
three-page audit shows coverage and reported deed shares separately.
